# Writing patterns from Gut Microbes research articles

Use these as functional patterns, not text templates. Never copy distinctive wording from published articles.

## Exemplar index

Choose recent articles matching the user's design, organism, assay, and evidence depth. Examples available at skill creation include:

- Human population microbiome survey: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2026.2694242
- Longitudinal microbiome dynamics: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2026.2659397
- Animal/host-pathophysiology experiment: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2026.2652474
- Microbial discovery and antitumor characterization: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2025.2599562
- Diet-microbiota evidence synthesis: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19490976.2024.2350785

When web access is available, update the exemplar set with 5-15 recent Research Articles. Prefer at least five close methodological matches. Separate reviews from primary research.

## Common section functions

### Introduction

1. Establish the host condition, biological process, or translational problem.
2. Summarize what is known about the relevant microbiota, metabolite, pathway, or intervention.
3. Define the unresolved gap without overstating novelty.
4. State the study design, objective, and evidence contributed.

### Results

Give each subsection one evidence job. Open with the question or comparison, report the result with figure/table linkage, and close with a restrained factual takeaway. Keep mechanistic interpretation for Discussion unless directly tested in that subsection.

### Discussion

1. State the principal findings without repeating the abstract.
2. Explain how the evidence advances or qualifies prior work.
3. Interpret plausible biology in proportion to evidence.
4. Address alternative explanations, confounding, and design limitations.
5. End with a specific, non-promotional implication.

## Study-type result sequences

### Human cohort or cross-sectional study

1. Cohort and clinical characteristics.
2. Community diversity and global composition.
3. Differential taxa or functions.
4. Associations with host phenotypes.
5. Adjusted, sensitivity, or validation analyses.

Do not imply causality from cross-sectional association.

### Longitudinal or intervention study

1. Participant/animal flow and baseline comparability.
2. Host or clinical response over time.
3. Microbiome change over time.
4. Taxonomic/functional features associated with response.
5. Robustness, mediation, or validation analyses when performed.

### Animal mechanistic study

1. Intervention changes the host phenotype.
2. Intervention changes microbiota or microbial function.
3. Candidate taxa/metabolites/pathways are identified.
4. Transfer, depletion, rescue, or targeted validation tests causality.
5. Molecular or physiological mechanism is tested.

Omit absent causal stages; never write around them as if completed.

### Multi-omics study

1. Cohort/model and primary phenotype.
2. Each omics layer's main signal.
3. Cross-omics integration.
4. Host association or functional interpretation.
5. Independent or experimental validation.

Distinguish exploratory integration from confirmatory validation.

## Abstract pattern

Use a compact unstructured abstract unless current instructions require otherwise:

1. Problem and gap.
2. Study design and essential methods.
3. Two to four principal quantitative findings.
4. Evidence-calibrated conclusion and implication.

Do not introduce a result, sample size, or mechanism absent from the main text.

## Title pattern

Prefer an informative title naming the central exposure/intervention, microbiome feature, host context, and supported relationship. Avoid causal verbs for observational designs and promotional adjectives such as `novel`, `breakthrough`, or `remarkable`.
